# The Jevons Dividend: sources

*By Srujan Valisetti.* The printed book gives a short reference for each figure and quotation. This page gives the full note behind each one: the source, where in it the figure or quotation is found, any calculation of our own, any caveat, and a web address where there is one. Notes are numbered as in the printed book, chapter by chapter. A few notes that back neither a figure nor a quotation were left out of the printed book; they are kept here, marked "not in the print edition".

## Chapter 1: The Coal Question

**Note 1.**


> It went the other way. Coal burned in Scotland's iron industry rose tenfold.

W. S. Jevons, *The Coal Question* (1865), ch. VII: "the reduction of the consumption of coal, per ton of iron, to less than one-third of its former amount, has been followed, in Scotland, by a ten-fold total consumption". Jevons credits the growth "in some part" to "the use of the hot blast in smelting". https://www.econlib.org/library/YPDBooks/Jevons/jvnCQ.html?chapter_num=9

**Note 2.**


> "It is wholly a confusion of ideas," he wrote, "to suppose that the economical use of fuel is equivalent to a diminished consumption. The very contrary is the truth."

Jevons, *The Coal Question*, ch. VII.

**Note 3.**


> His reasoning was simple once you see it. When iron needs less coal, iron gets cheaper. Cheaper iron finds new uses, and each of those uses burns coal of its own. Jevons pointed to "the indirect effect of cheap iron in accelerating other coal-consuming branches of industry."

Jevons, *The Coal Question*, ch. VII.

**Note 4.**


> He didn't think coal was special. "As a rule," he wrote, "new modes of economy will lead to an increase of consumption according to a principle recognised in many parallel instances."

Jevons, *The Coal Question*, ch. VII.

**Note 5.**


> In November 2022, asking an AI model to do work at the level of the original ChatGPT cost about $20 for every million tokens, the small pieces of text these models read and write. By October 2024, a model with the same score on a standard test of general knowledge cost 7 cents for the same million tokens.

Stanford HAI, *AI Index Report 2025*: from $20.00 per million tokens (November 2022) to $0.07 (October 2024, Gemini-1.5-Flash-8B) for a model scoring 64.8 on the MMLU benchmark. https://hai-production.s3.amazonaws.com/files/hai_ai_index_report_2025.pdf

**Note 6.**


> At a higher level of ability, the fall was steeper still. The price of a model that could pass a test of graduate-level science questions more than half the time went from $15 per million tokens in May 2024 to 12 cents in December of the same year.

*AI Index Report 2025*: the GPQA benchmark above 50%, from $15 per million tokens (May 2024) to $0.12 (December 2024, Phi 4).

**Note 7.**


> It isn't a one-off. Researchers who track these prices estimate that, depending on the task, the cost of running AI models has been falling somewhere between 9 and 900 times a year.

Epoch AI estimate, quoted in the *AI Index Report 2025*.

**Note 8.**


> So what happened to use? If Jevons was right, falling prices should bring a flood of new demand, and they have. In July 2025, Google said it was processing 980 trillion tokens a month across its products. On 29 October, Sundar Pichai told investors the figure had passed 1.3 quadrillion, "more than 20x growth in a year." He added that customers using Google's models directly were sending seven billion tokens a minute, and that the Gemini app had more than 650 million users a month.

Alphabet Q3 2025 earnings call, 29 October 2025. https://s206.q4cdn.com/479360582/files/doc_events/2025/Oct/29/2025_Q3_Earnings_Transcript.pdf

**Not in the print edition.**


> Take those numbers with some salt. They are a company's own figures. Critics point out that newer models which "think" before answering produce many tokens nobody reads, so raw token counts flatter real use.

A critic's view: The Decoder, "Google boasts 1.3 quadrillion tokens each month, but the figure is mostly window dressing". https://the-decoder.com/google-boasts-1-3-quadrillion-tokens-each-month-but-the-figure-is-mostly-window-dressing/

**Note 9.**


> A careful review of this research, published in 2016 by the economists Gillingham, Rapson and Wagner, found "little support" for the idea that efficiency usually backfires. For driving and electricity, most of the studies they reviewed found that extra use ate up between 5% and 25% of the expected saving. Counting the knock-on effects on what people buy with the money saved, they put the total at about 20% to 40%. Their own judgement was that in most cases it wouldn't go above about 60%, and most measured cases were well below that.

Gillingham, Rapson and Wagner, "The Rebound Effect and Energy Efficiency Policy", *Review of Environmental Economics and Policy* 10(1), 2016. Figures from the working-paper version (2015). https://files.gwagner.com/production/uploads/Rebound_Effect_GRW.pdf

**Note 10.**


> That is the whole rule. The economist James Bessen put it this way: "If demand increases sufficiently in response to productivity-improving technology, then employment will grow; otherwise, it will fall."

J. Bessen, "Automation and Jobs: When Technology Boosts Employment", *Economic Policy* (2019). Quoted from the April 2019 panel version. http://www.economic-policy.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/997_Automation-and-Jobs.pdf

**Note 11.**


> Three Stanford economists studying AI and jobs today, Erik Brynjolfsson and his colleagues Chandar and Chen, make the same point in more technical language: higher productivity in a job "could lead to either reduced or increased employment, depending on, among other things, how elastic demand is for the output of those workers."

Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen, "Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence", Stanford Digital Economy Lab, August 2026 update, footnote 2. https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Canaries_BrynjolfssonChandarChen.pdf

**Note 12.**


> Bessen tested the rule against history. He traced three American industries over two centuries: cotton textiles, steel and cars. The numbers are large. From 1810 to 2011, output per hour of work in American cotton textiles rose more than 800-fold. The economy as a whole grew too, but output per person in the whole economy rose only 30-fold over the same years.

Bessen (2019): "from 1810 to 2011, real GDP per capita rose 30-fold, but output per hour in cotton textiles rose over 800-fold."

**Note 13.**


> Then each market filled up. By about 1950, demand in all three industries had stopped responding the way it used to. From then on, over the following decades, new machines came to cost jobs in those industries instead of adding them.

Bessen (2019): estimates of the elasticity of demand were "significantly greater than one" in the early years (1820 textiles, 1870 steel, 1910 cars) and "all are less than one" by 1950 (1951 for cars).

**Note 14.**


> Bessen draws a careful lesson. In the near future, he writes, the main effect of automation may be "a major reallocation of jobs even if it does not permanently eliminate large numbers of jobs." And he adds: "This kind of change can be highly disruptive nevertheless."

Bessen (2019).

**Note 15.**


> Other economists have measured the balance across the whole American economy. Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo split the effect of technology into two forces: automation, which takes tasks away from people, and new tasks, which give people work they never did before. From 1947 to 1987, the two were almost exactly balanced. Automation cut demand for workers by about 0.48% a year, and new tasks added back about 0.47%. From 1987 to 2017 the balance tipped. Automation took about 0.7% a year, and new tasks added back only 0.35%.

D. Acemoglu and P. Restrepo, "Automation and New Tasks: How Technology Displaces and Reinstates Labor", *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 33(2), 2019. Figures from NBER Working Paper 25684 (2019). https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25684/w25684.pdf

**Note 16.**


> New work does keep arriving. In 2022 the economist David Autor and three colleagues went through American census records back to 1940. They asked a simple question: how many people in 2018 worked in jobs that didn't exist in 1940? Their answer was about 60%.

Autor, Chin, Salomons and Seegmiller, "New Frontiers: The Origins and Content of New Work, 1940–2018", NBER Working Paper 30389 (2022). https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30389/w30389.pdf

**Note 17.**


> Their study ends with the same warning as Acemoglu and Restrepo's. Since about 1980, they found, the pressure that removes old work has grown stronger, and the pull that creates new work has not.

Autor et al. (2022), conclusion: "the demand-eroding effects of automation innovations have intensified in the last four decades while the demand-increasing effects of augmentation innovations have not."

**Note 18.**


> Across whole economies, there is no sign yet of mass job loss. Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen, working from the records of the payroll firm ADP, find "no evidence of widespread, economy-wide job displacement." In Denmark, where adoption surveys can be linked to official records, the economists Humlum and Vestergaard could rule out effects on pay or hours worked larger than 2%, two years after ChatGPT arrived.

Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen (August 2026 update), fact 1. A. Humlum and E. Vestergaard, "Still Waters, Rapid Currents: Early Labor Market Transformation under Generative AI", NBER Working Paper 33777 (revised March 2026). https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w33777/w33777.pdf

**Note 19.**


> But one group is already feeling it. In the Stanford study's latest update, people aged 22 to 25 in the jobs most exposed to AI are 19% fewer than they would be if they had kept pace with their peers in less exposed jobs. Experienced workers show no such gap. The fall comes mostly from companies hiring fewer young people; the authors find much less change in people leaving or being let go. The authors are careful to call these early signals, "canaries in the coal mine", and they do not claim AI caused them.

Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen (August 2026 update), facts 2, 4 and 5. The 19% is the shortfall against the level employment would have reached had it kept pace with less-exposed peers.

**Note 20.**


> Every story in this book assumes that people keep doing some kinds of work better than machines, or more cheaply, or in ways customers prefer. If a machine could one day do every task a person can, the history in this book would stop being a guide. Two economists who have modelled that possibility, Korinek and Suh, describe both ends. If there is a limit to how complex the tasks people can do are, and machines reach full automation, wages collapse. "If automation proceeds sufficiently slowly," they also write, "then there is always enough work for humans, and wages may rise forever."

A. Korinek and D. Suh, "Scenarios for the Transition to AGI", NBER Working Paper 32255 (2024). https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32255/w32255.pdf

## Chapter 2: The Machine That Hired Tellers

**Note 1.**


> In July 1982, the US Bureau of Labor Statistics looked back at a forecast it had made about bank tellers. It had expected about 337,000 of them to be at work in 1980. The actual count was about 506,000, half as many again.

US Bureau of Labor Statistics, *Monthly Labor Review*, July 1982, table 2: tellers 1980 about 506,000; BLS had projected 337,000 for 1980. https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/1982/07/art4full.pdf

**Note 2.**


> The forecasters had badly underrated a job that was growing fast. The census, which counts a little differently, put bank tellers at about 284,000 in 1970 and about 509,000 in 1980, a rise of nearly 80% in ten years.

US Census Bureau, 1980 Census, PC80-S1-15, table 1: bank tellers in the experienced civilian labor force, 284,413 (1970, recoded to 1980 occupations) and 509,449 (1980), up 79.1% (our calculation, `analysis/results.json`). This count includes the unemployed and is not comparable with the BLS figures. https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/1984/demo/pc80-s1-15.pdf

**Note 3.**


> By then the industry was installing a machine built to do much of what those people did. Automated teller machines first appeared in American banks in the 1970s, and they handled some of the commonest tasks at the counter, "such as dispensing cash and taking deposits."

J. Bessen, "Toil and Technology", IMF *Finance & Development* 52(1), March 2015. https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2015/03/bessen.htm

**Note 4.**


> In 1980 the country had about 19,000 of them.

Federal Reserve Bulletin (2009), "Households' Access to and Use of Electronic Banking", table 1 ("ATMs and bank branches", thousands). https://www.federalreserve.gov/Pubs/Bulletin/2009/articles/onlinebanking/dlink/fig1.htm

**Note 5.**


> For its first dozen years the machine spread slowly. From 1980 to 1993 banks added about 6,000 ATMs a year. Then the curve turned steep. From 1993 to 2001 they added nearly 29,000 a year, and the count went from about 95,000 to about 324,000. By 2007 it stood at about 415,000, some 22 times the 1980 figure.

Federal Reserve Bulletin (2009), table 1: ATMs 1980 19,000; 1990 80,000; 1993 95,000; 2000 273,000; 2001 324,000; 2007 415,000. Yearly additions and the 22-fold rise are our calculations (`analysis/results.json`). https://www.federalreserve.gov/Pubs/Bulletin/2009/articles/onlinebanking/dlink/fig1.htm

**Note 6.**


> Bessen, whose rule chapter 1 quoted, dates the same turn: "Starting in the mid-1990s, banks rapidly increased their use of ATMs."

Bessen, "Toil and Technology" (2015). https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2015/03/bessen.htm

**Note 7.**


> Yet the number of tellers rose. The government's survey of jobs counted about 493,000 tellers in 2000 and about 608,000 in 2007.

BLS Occupational Employment Statistics, tellers (SOC 43-3071): 2000, 492,950; May 2007, 607,960. BLS warns that these surveys are not designed for comparisons over time; the counts cover wage and salary jobs. Series in `analysis/data/tellers.csv`, from Wayback Machine copies of https://www.bls.gov/oes/tables.htm

**Note 8.**


> The survey only starts in 1999, so it can't speak for the 1980s and most of the 1990s, and its counts can't be set against the census figures from 1980, which were made a different way. For those years the evidence is Bessen's. Working from census samples, he found that "the number of bank teller jobs did not decrease as the ATMs were rolled out."

Bessen, "Toil and Technology" (2015), https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2015/03/bessen.htm . His teller series is built from census and American Community Survey samples: J. Bessen, Boston University School of Law Working Paper 15-49, working-paper version. https://www.bu.edu/law/files/2016/01/NewTech-012020161.pdf

**Note 9.**


> Bessen found the answer in the cost of a branch. The machines cut the number of tellers an urban branch needed, from about 20 in 1988 to about 13 in 2004. A branch with 13 tellers costs less to run than one with 20. Banks answered by "opening more branches to compete for greater market share," and over the same period, as he reports it, urban branches grew in number by 43%. In his words: "Fewer tellers were required for each branch, but more branches meant that teller jobs did not disappear."

Bessen, "Toil and Technology" (2015). The article gives 1988 to 2004 for the tellers per branch; the 43% follows in the same passage, with no separate dates. https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2015/03/bessen.htm

**Note 10.**


> The national branch count moved the same way. Bank branches numbered about 39,000 in 1980, about 51,000 by 1990, about 65,000 by 2000 and about 79,000 by 2007, on the Federal Reserve's count.

Federal Reserve Bulletin (2009), table 1: branches 1980 about 39,000; 1990 about 51,000; 2000 about 65,000; 2007 about 79,000. https://www.federalreserve.gov/Pubs/Bulletin/2009/articles/onlinebanking/dlink/fig1.htm

**Note 11.**


> Bessen draws the general lesson in one line. The data, he writes, show "technology today largely displacing workers to new jobs, not replacing them entirely."

Bessen, "Toil and Technology" (2015). https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2015/03/bessen.htm

**Note 12.**


> The job that survived was a different job. Bessen describes two forces that kept tellers employed. The first was the cheaper branch. The second was that "while ATMs automated some tasks, the remaining tasks that were not automated became more valuable." As banks fought for customers, "tellers became an important part of the 'relationship banking team.'" His verdict on the whole episode: "the economic response to automation of bank tellers' work was much more dynamic than many people would expect."

Bessen, "Toil and Technology" (2015). https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2015/03/bessen.htm

**Note 13.**


> He sums up the change in one sentence: "The skills of the teller changed: cash handling became less important and human interaction more important."

Bessen, "Toil and Technology" (2015). https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2015/03/bessen.htm

**Not in the print edition.**


> Tellers spent less time counting notes and more time with customers, moving toward selling and relationship work.

Bessen, "Toil and Technology" (2015), on tellers in the "relationship banking team"; and Bessen, BU School of Law Working Paper 15-49, working-paper version. https://www.bu.edu/law/files/2016/01/NewTech-012020161.pdf

**Not in the print edition.**


> That shift is the quieter half of act one. The machine took the part of the job that could be written down as steps, and the part that was left, talking to people and working out what they needed, became the reason to keep a person at the counter. Bessen's book *Learning by Doing* (2015) makes a wider point that fits here: the skills a new technology calls for are often learned on the job, over years.

J. Bessen, *Learning by Doing: The Real Connection between Innovation, Wages, and Wealth* (Yale University Press, 2015), publisher's description. https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300195668/learning-by-doing/

**Note 14.**


> A caution belongs here too. Much of act one rests on one economist's analysis. The book's own data can't test it for the years before 1999, and banks may have opened branches for reasons this chapter doesn't cover. Branches were already multiplying before the machines arrived: on the FDIC's count of commercial-bank branches, they rose from about 22,000 in 1970 to about 38,000 in 1980, by close to 80%, when ATMs were still rare.

FDIC Historical Statistics on Banking, commercial-bank branches at year end: 21,597 (1970) and 38,458 (1980), +78.1% (our calculation, `analysis/results.json`). https://api.fdic.gov/banks/summary?filters=STNAME:%22U.S.%20States%20and%20DC%22

**Note 15.**


> After 2007, teller jobs began to fall, and they kept falling. In 2010 there were about 556,000. By 2023, about 341,000. By May 2025, about 329,000.

BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, tellers: May 2010, 556,310; May 2023, 340,820 (https://www.bls.gov/oes/2023/may/oes433071.htm); May 2025, 329,480 (BLS API). 2007 to 2025: about −46% (our calculation, `analysis/results.json`).

**Note 16.**


> At first the branches stayed open and the counters thinned. Between 2007 and 2012, teller jobs fell by about 11%, while the number of commercial-bank branches still grew by about 6%.

Our calculation from `analysis/data/tellers.csv` (in `analysis/results.json`): OEWS tellers 607,960 (2007) to 541,770 (2012), −10.9%; FDIC commercial-bank branches 77,968 to 82,461, +5.8%; then a peak of 82,461 (2012) and 68,860 (2025), −16.5%. FDIC Historical Statistics on Banking, year-end. https://api.fdic.gov/banks/summary?filters=STNAME:%22U.S.%20States%20and%20DC%22

**Note 17.**


> The Federal Reserve's table stops in 2007, and the FDIC, which insures bank deposits, counts in other ways, so their numbers can't be joined into one line. On the FDIC's widest count, the offices run by all insured banks and savings institutions, the total peaked at about 99,500 in June 2009 and fell to just over 76,000 by June 2024, about a quarter fewer.

FDIC Summary of Deposits, offices operated by FDIC-insured institutions: 99,540 in June 2009, 86,364 in June 2019 (FDIC Quarterly 14(1), 2020, https://www.fdic.gov/analysis/quarterly-banking-profile/fdic-quarterly/2020-vol14-1/fdic-v14n1-4q2019-article.pdf); "more than 76,000" domestic offices in June 2024 (FDIC press release, 20 September 2024, https://www.fdic.gov/news/press-releases/2024/fdic-releases-results-summary-deposits-annual-survey). The fall of about 24% is our calculation.

**Note 18.**


> Some of the fall came in hard years for banks. It began in 2008, the year of the financial crisis. The steepest one-year drop in the survey, about 14%, came between 2020 and 2021, in the pandemic and in the years when the survey was moving to new job codes.

OEWS tellers 541,770 (2012) to 423,570 (May 2020), lower in every year between; 423,570 to 364,210 (May 2021), −14.0%, the largest one-year fall in the series (our calculation, `analysis/results.json`). OEWS moved to the 2018 occupation codes over May 2019 to 2021; the teller code did not change (`analysis/README.md`). https://www.bls.gov/oes/tables.htm

**Not in the print edition.**


> What changed? Looking ahead, the Bureau of Labor Statistics names the forces it expects to keep cutting teller jobs: online and mobile banking, video kiosks, smarter ATMs, and fewer branches.

BLS *Occupational Outlook Handbook*, Tellers. https://www.bls.gov/ooh/office-and-administrative-support/tellers.htm

**Note 19.**


> And the forecast now runs the other way from 1982. The Bureau of Labor Statistics expects teller jobs to fall by another 13% between 2025 and 2035, a loss of some 44,700 jobs. Its projections use a slightly wider count, which puts 2025 at about 339,000.

BLS *Occupational Outlook Handbook*, Tellers: projected change 2025–35, −13% (−44,700 jobs from 339,200). The projections use the BLS National Employment Matrix, which also counts the self-employed, so 2025 differs from the OEWS figure. https://www.bls.gov/ooh/office-and-administrative-support/tellers.htm

**Note 20.**


> One more number matters. From 1990 to 2025, total employment in American commercial banks barely moved, staying between about 1.33 and 1.45 million people on annual averages.

BLS Current Employment Statistics, commercial banking employees (CEU5552211001), annual averages, 1990–2025: lowest 1,327,500 (2000), highest 1,450,800 (1990) (`analysis/results.json`). https://data.bls.gov/timeseries/CEU5552211001

**Not in the print edition.**


> Employers expect more of the same. In the World Economic Forum's 2025 survey of employers, bank tellers are among the roles expected to shrink fastest by 2030.

World Economic Forum, *Future of Jobs Report 2025* (January 2025), employer survey, 2025–2030. https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Future_of_Jobs_Report_2025.pdf

**Note 21.**


> That is a forecast by the companies themselves, not a measurement. The same survey asked what employers plan to do when their staff's skills become less relevant. Half said they would move people from declining roles to growing ones, and 40% said they would cut staff.

World Economic Forum, *Future of Jobs Report 2025*: "50% planning to transition staff from declining to growing roles" and "40% planning to reduce staff as their skills become less relevant" (employer plans). https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Future_of_Jobs_Report_2025.pdf

**Note 22.**


> Acemoglu and Restrepo, the economists whose measurements of automation appeared in chapter 1, warn about what they call "so-so technologies". These are machines that replace people while making only small gains in productivity. "It is not the 'brilliant' automation technologies that threaten employment and wages," they write, "but 'so-so technologies' that generate small productivity improvements." One of their examples is automated customer service, which they describe as "generally deemed to be low-quality."

D. Acemoglu and P. Restrepo, "Automation and New Tasks" (2019), NBER Working Paper 25684. https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25684/w25684.pdf

**Note 23.**


> Bessen answers that the quality of the machine isn't what decides the result. "'Brilliant' technologies will lead to declining employment if demand is inelastic," he writes, "and 'so-so' technologies can lead to job growth if demand is highly elastic."

J. Bessen, "Automation and Jobs: When Technology Boosts Employment" (2019), April 2019 panel version. http://www.economic-policy.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/997_Automation-and-Jobs.pdf

**Note 24.**


> Shop checkouts tell a similar story. Barcode scanners spread through American shops in the 1970s and 1980s, and one study found that early scanners raised a store's labour productivity by only about 4.5%, a modest gain.

E. Basker, "Raising the Barcode Scanner: Technology and Productivity in the Retail Sector", NBER Working Paper 17825, working-paper version (Census of Retail Trade, 1972, 1977 and 1982). https://www.nber.org/papers/w17825

**Note 25.**


> Cashier jobs grew anyway. The census counted about 962,000 cashiers in 1970 and about 1.88 million in 1980. BLS counted about 3.29 million in 2000, and its job survey found about 3.64 million in 2018.

US Census Bureau, PC80-S1-15, table 1: cashiers 961,807 (1970) and 1,875,810 (1980), https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/1984/demo/pc80-s1-15.pdf ; BLS, *The Economics Daily*, May 2003: 3.29 million in 2000, https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2003/may/wk3/art05.htm ; BLS OEWS, cashiers (SOC 41-2011), May 2018: 3,635,550, https://www.bls.gov/oes/2018/may/oes412011.htm

**Note 26.**


> These are separate counts and shouldn't be joined, but the direction is plain. Since 2018 the survey shows a fall, to about 3.09 million in 2025, about 15%, and BLS expects a further 6% drop by 2035, naming self-checkout among the reasons.

BLS OEWS, cashiers, May 2025: 3,089,410 (BLS API; −15% from 2018, our calculation); BLS *Occupational Outlook Handbook*, Cashiers: projected −6%, 2025–35. https://www.bls.gov/ooh/sales/cashiers.htm

**Note 27.**


> The same split shows up in customer service itself. Acemoglu and Restrepo's so-so example replaces the agent. In a study chapter 5 looks at closely, Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond gave 5,172 support agents an AI assistant instead, and issues resolved per hour rose by about 15%, while fewer of the newer agents left.

E. Brynjolfsson, D. Li and L. R. Raymond, "Generative AI at Work", *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 140(2), 2025. https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/140/2/889/7756313

## Chapter 3: Hungry Demand, and When It Fills

**Note 1.**


> In 1920, about one in every fifty American working women was a telephone operator.

J. Feigenbaum and D. P. Gross, "Answering the Call of Automation: How the Labor Market Adjusted to Mechanizing Telephone Operation", *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 139(3), 2024. Figures and quotations from NBER Working Paper 28061, working-paper version (revised February 2024), p. 6: operators were "roughly 2% of the U.S. female workforce" in 1920. https://www.nber.org/papers/w28061

**Not in the print edition.**


> The work was hard to scale. Each new subscriber added a whole set of new possible connections, and as the network grew, the number of operators needed to keep up with the calls swelled.

Feigenbaum and Gross (NBER w28061, 2024), p. 6. https://www.nber.org/papers/w28061

**Note 2.**


> By the 1920s the American telephone industry served nearly 15 million phones and connected more than 65 million calls a day. AT&T, the dominant company, had become the largest employer in the country, and about half of its workforce were operators.

Feigenbaum and Gross (NBER w28061, 2024), pp. 5 and 7, citing their Appendix Table A.1: "nearly 15 million telephones" and "more than 65 million calls per day" in the 1920s; AT&T the largest US employer by the early 1920s, "with telephone operators comprising around half of its workforce." https://www.nber.org/papers/w28061

**Note 3.**


> Operators employed by the telephone companies grew from about 73,000 in 1910 to about 135,000 in 1920 and about 182,000 in 1930.

Feigenbaum and Gross (NBER w28061, 2024), table II: operators in the telephone industry (complete-count census), 1910, 73,030; 1920, 134,630; 1930, 182,040 (`analysis/data/telephone_operators.csv`). https://www.nber.org/papers/w28061

**Note 4.**


> Then AT&T set out to take the operator out of the local call. In 1917, AT&T's engineering department recommended mechanical switching for local service in large cities, and its own records projected that the new equipment would cut the number of operators there by up to 70 to 80%.

Feigenbaum and Gross (NBER w28061, 2024), p. 7, citing Gherardi (1917): AT&T records "projected that the automatic equipment would reduce the number of operators in large cities by up to 70 to 80%." https://www.nber.org/papers/w28061

**Note 5.**


> Adoption began in the late 1910s and sped up. About 32% of Bell telephones were on dial by 1930 and 60% by 1940. The last of them were converted in 1978.

> The company rolled the tool out in stages, to different groups of agents at different times. > **Step 7. Turn the evidence into a volume.** Evidence usually comes as an elasticity, the percentage change in volume for each 1% change in price. For a small cut, multiply: at −0.5, each 1% off the price brings about 0.5% more volume. For a cut as large as AI may allow, use the exact form: the volume multiple is (1 − the cut) raised to the power of the elasticity. In a spreadsheet, a 25% cut at −0.55 is =0.75^-0.55, which gives about 1.17. Run it backwards to see what your bar demands: the elasticity you need is =LN(C)/LN(1-D), with D as a fraction.

Our calculation, using the standard constant-elasticity form: volume multiple = (1 − cut) ^ elasticity. At a 25% cut, −0.55 gives 0.75^−0.55 ≈ 1.17; the elasticity needed for 1.67 times is ln(1.67) ÷ ln(0.75) ≈ −1.78. Elasticities from note 3 (Tellis) and note 4 (Uber). ## Chapter 9: Augment Before You Automate

**Note 1.**


> In 2023, 758 consultants at Boston Consulting Group took part in an experiment, about 7% of the firm's consultants at the individual-contributor level. The researchers split them into three groups: one worked without AI, one was given GPT-4, and one was given GPT-4 along with a short overview of how to prompt it.

Dell'Acqua, McFowland, Mollick, Lifshitz-Assaf, Kellogg, Rajendran, Krayer, Candelon and Lakhani, "Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier: Field Experimental Evidence of the Effects of AI on Knowledge Worker Productivity and Quality", Harvard Business School Working Paper 24-013, 22 September 2023 (SSRN 4573321), abstract: sample, pre-registration and the three groups. https://mitsloan.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2023-10/SSRN-id4573321.pdf

**Note 2.**


> On a set of 18 realistic consulting tasks, the consultants with AI did well. They completed 12.2% more tasks and finished them 25.1% more quickly. In the paper's headline figure, their work scored more than 40% higher on quality than the group without AI.

Dell'Acqua et al. (2023), abstract: 18 tasks, 12.2%, 25.1% and "more than 40% higher quality compared to a control group".

**Note 3.**


> Then came a different kind of task, one the researchers placed outside what the AI of the day did well. Without AI, consultants got it right 84.5% of the time. With AI, they got it right about 60% or 70% of the time, depending on the group. Both AI groups did worse. The group that had been taught to prompt fell 24 percentage points below the consultants working alone, and the group with GPT-4 alone fell 13; the gap between the two groups is small enough that the authors give it only modest statistical weight. And on this task the AI users still finished faster. In the group with the prompting overview, they spent more than 11 minutes less, a cut of 30%.

Dell'Acqua et al. (2023), section on the task outside the frontier and Table 4: 84.5% for the control group against about 60% and 70%, down 24 percentage points (GPT-4 with overview) and 13 (GPT-4 only); the difference between the two AI groups is significant only at the 10% level. Time: Table 5 discussion.

**Note 4.**


> First, the gains inside the edge were largest for the weaker consultants. Those below the average performance threshold improved by 43% and those above it by 17%, each measured against their own scores.

Dell'Acqua et al. (2023), abstract.

**Note 5.**


> Second, the work became more alike. The authors warn that while AI helps produce better content, it "might lead to more homogenized outputs," with less variety in the ideas.

Dell'Acqua et al. (2023), results and Appendix D.

**Note 6.**


> Third, people copied. The group given the prompting overview kept more of GPT-4's text unchanged than the group given GPT-4 alone.

Dell'Acqua et al. (2023), Appendix C.

**Note 7.**


> Two cautions about the source. The figures above come from the 2023 working paper. A version was published in *Organization Science* in March 2026, and its abstract keeps the 12.2% and 25.1% gains and "significantly improved quality," but drops the 40% quality figure and the 43% and 17% split.

The same nine authors, *Organization Science* 37(2): 403-423, March 2026, DOI 10.1287/orsc.2025.21838; abstract and metadata via Crossref. The journal's full text could not be read. https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1287/orsc.2025.21838

Dell'Acqua et al. (2023), title page.

**Note 9.**


> The authors also looked at the consultants who used AI and still got the hard task right, and found two styles of working. They called some consultants "centaurs," after the creature that is half human and half horse. These consultants kept a "strategic division of labor between humans and machines," and switched between AI and their own work, "allocating responsibilities based on the strengths and capabilities of each entity." Others they called "cyborgs," who "intertwine their efforts with AI at the very frontier of capabilities," handing work back and forth within a single task, starting a sentence, say, for the AI to finish.

Dell'Acqua et al. (2023), section on patterns of use: the strategies of consultants who answered the outside-the-frontier task correctly while using AI.

**Note 10.**


> A second study shows the same pattern far from any consulting firm. Otis and colleagues gave Kenyan entrepreneurs access to an AI assistant built on GPT-4, reached through WhatsApp, and compared them with a group who didn't get it. The trial covered 640 business owners and was published in *Management Science* in July 2026.

Otis et al., randomised trial with 640 Kenyan entrepreneurs, *Management Science*, published online 10 July 2026, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2024.06909. https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1287/mnsc.2024.06909 ; author copy: https://escholarship.org/content/qt0fb001r8/qt0fb001r8.pdf

**Note 11.**


> The trial found no measurable average effect. The authors were "unable to reject the null hypothesis of no average treatment effect" on revenues and profits. Underneath the average, the two groups appear to have moved in opposite directions. In the authors' subsample analyses, low performers "did nearly 10% worse because of the AI assistant," while high performers "may have benefited by over 15%." The difference lay in the advice each group chose to act on.

Otis et al. (2026), abstract and author copy (note 10).

**Note 12.**


> There is a slower kind of harm too. Budzyń and colleagues looked at doctors in four Polish centres who had begun using AI to help them find adenomas, a kind of growth, during colonoscopy. They then compared the doctors' standard procedures, done without AI, before and after the AI arrived. Before, adenomas were found in 28.4% of standard colonoscopies (226 of 795). After, the figure was 22.4% (145 of 648).

Budzyń et al., *Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology* 2025;10(10):896-903, abstract (PubMed 40816301). Observational study in four Polish centres; an erratum and letters have been published. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40816301/

**Note 13.**


> Chapter 2 promised a return to Acemoglu and Restrepo's warning about so-so machines, and this is where it belongs. Their examples of so-so technology include "several of the applications of artificial intelligence technology to tasks that are currently challenging for machines."

D. Acemoglu and P. Restrepo, "Automation and New Tasks: How Technology Displaces and Reinstates Labor", *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 33(2): 3-30, 2019; version read: NBER Working Paper 25684, March 2019. https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25684/w25684.pdf

**Note 14.**


> An OECD study by Milanez looked at 96 cases of AI being put to work in manufacturing and finance across eight countries, in 2021 and 2022. The summary: "job reorganisation appears more prevalent than job displacement," with automation turning jobs "towards tasks in which humans have a comparative advantage." In 77% of the cases, the AI had no reported effect on the number of workers most affected. In the other 23%, jobs fell, but the firms didn't make people redundant. They moved them to other roles or business areas, or let numbers fall through slower hiring and people leaving. In 20% of the cases, hiring had slowed or was expected to. In 30%, jobs connected with building and running the AI were growing.

Milanez, "The impact of AI on the workplace: Evidence from OECD case studies of AI implementation", OECD Social, Employment and Migration Working Papers No. 289, 2023. https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2023/03/the-impact-of-ai-on-the-workplace-evidence-from-oecd-case-studies-of-ai-implementation_b4c2c6ee/2247ce58-en.pdf

**Note 15.**


> We read one finding there as the Jevons pattern at the scale of a single workplace. When AI automated a task it could do more quickly or cheaply, Milanez writes, "demand for human workers to do the other tasks around the AI rose."

Milanez (2023), note 14.

**Note 16.**

## Chapter 11: Keep the Ladder

**Note 1.**


> On 22 September 2025 the internet company Cloudflare announced on its blog that it would hire as many as 1,111 interns over the course of 2026. The post named the trend it was answering. > Hosseini and Lichtinger, two economists at Harvard, followed the online résumés of 65 million workers at more than 280,000 American firms from 2015 to 2025. They picked out the firms that had adopted generative AI and compared them with similar firms that had not. In the version of their paper dated May 2026, junior employment at the adopters fell by about 9% after six quarters, counted from the start of 2023, relative to the comparison firms. **Note 1.**


> On 31 July 2026, three researchers from the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis published a count of what company bosses say about AI. Ozkan, Kalyani and Sullivan had gone through about 490,000 earnings-call transcripts from 5,198 firms, covering 2000 to 2025, picked out every sentence about productivity, and then had an AI model sort them. By the last months of 2025, AI came up in roughly 15% of those sentences. About 95% of the productivity sentences that mentioned AI were about gains still to come. When the same executives talked about productivity from anything else, about three-quarters of the sentences looked ahead.

Seventy-eight per cent of their firms used it, on a count that gives big employers more weight in four of the seven countries. The executives said AI had raised their annual labour productivity growth by 0.2% over the last three years, "a modest impact", and they expected a boost of 0.6% a year over the next three.

**Note 6.**


> In 1899, David wrote, electric motors were "less than 5 percent of factory mechanical drive" in American industry. It took about two more decades for half of factory drive to be electrified, and factory productivity didn't show the effect until the early 1920s, "four decades after the first central power station opened for business." When it came, it came hard: about half of a 5-percentage-point jump in the growth of manufacturing productivity in the 1920s was accounted for, statistically, by the growth of electric motors in factories.

P. A. David, "The Dynamo and the Computer: An Historical Perspective on the Modern Productivity Paradox", *American Economic Review* 80(2), May 1990, pp. 355-361. Quoted from a scanned copy of the journal pages, including David's "group drive" and "unit drive". https://www.gwern.net/doc/economics/automation/1990-david.pdf

**Note 7.**


> The delay had a cause. Most factories first added electric motors to their old shafts and belts. The owners saved some power, but the old equipment stayed in place, and measured productivity barely moved. The big gain came with "unit drive", in David's term: a motor on each machine, which let factories be rebuilt on one floor and laid out around the work. The economists Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson put it this way: "it took a generation for the nature of factory layouts to be re-invented."

Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson, NBER w25148 (revised January 2020). https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25148/w25148.pdf

**Note 8.**


> The same three economists argue that technologies "such as AI" need large hidden investments in new processes, new products, new business models and new skills, and those are "often intangible and poorly measured." So productivity looks lower than it is at first and higher later, when those investments pay off. They call the shape a J-curve.

Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson, NBER w25148, abstract; published in *American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics* 13(1), 2021 (the published text was not read). https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25148/w25148.pdf

McElheran, Yang, Kroff and Brynjolfsson, "The Rise of Industrial AI in America: Microfoundations of the Productivity J-curve(s)", preliminary draft, December 2025, US Census data on manufacturing, 2017 and 2021.
